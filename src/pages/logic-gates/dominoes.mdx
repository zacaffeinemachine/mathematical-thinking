Read the last page again and notice what its argument never used. It
never used current, or copper, or voltage. It used only this: each wire
is in one of two states, each gate obeys a fixed rule, and AND and OR
happen to send all-$\texttt{0}$s to $\texttt{0}$. Any material at all
with two states and a fixed rule will do just as well.

People have taken this literally. Working gates have been built out of
lines of standing dominoes, out of marbles rolling down a pegboard and
being steered left or right, out of water running through pipes and
valves, out of blocks inside the game *Minecraft*, and — in one
published experiment — out of a swarm of soldier crabs, which merge and
split as they flow down a corridor. None of these is a joke. Dominoes
have been laid out by the ten thousand to add two numbers together, and
the arrangement is the same arrangement an engineer would etch into
silicon.

This page is that claim, made playable.

A word about what the pictures are and are not. The logic underneath
every board below is simulated exactly: a wave of toppling spreads one
square per tick, and which tiles fall, in what order, is computed rather
than drawn by hand. The *wood* is not simulated. There is no gravity in
here, no friction, no tile that bounces or lands short. Think of each
board as an honest diagram of a real arrangement, drawn at an angle so
that you can see the tiles lean, rather than as footage of one. If you
would like to see the real thing, filmed builds of domino adders and
domino logic gates are easy to find, and worth ten minutes.

has to carry any logic. Press Play.

## OR — two runs, one continuation

The domino OR takes about ten seconds to understand. Run two lines of
dominoes into a single continuation line. If the left line topples, the
continuation topples. If the right line topples, the continuation
topples. If neither does, nothing happens.

Click a knob to set an input, then press Play. Clicking a row of the
table sets the inputs to that row.

<DominoDemo client:visible demo="or" />

That is the generous committee, in wood, with no current anywhere.

## The wall

Now the sting. Here is a much more elaborate arrangement — loops,
branches, the long way round — and it is worth being suspicious of it,
because it is going to lose an argument in a moment.

<DominoDemo client:visible demo="push-only" />

Set both inputs to $\texttt{0}$ and press Play. Nothing happens. Not
"almost nothing" — nothing at all, because a standing domino has no
reason to fall unless something knocks it.

That is not a fact about *this* board. Call a medium **push-only** if
nothing at all happens in it when nothing arrives: standing dominoes
stay standing, still water stays still, a marble in the tray stays in
the tray. In a push-only medium, feed every input $\texttt{0}$, and
every part of the gadget stays $\texttt{0}$, so the output is
$\texttt{0}$.

So no arrangement of dominoes, however large and however clever, is a
NOT gate. A NOT gate has to answer $\texttt{1}$ when its input is
$\texttt{0}$, and a board of standing dominoes with nothing arriving
cannot answer $\texttt{1}$ to anything. This is exactly the politeness
of AND and OR from the previous page, wearing a different coat.

## NOT — and what it costs

Domino builders know this and pay the price the argument asked for. To
get a flipper, they run one extra line that topples *on its own*,
whatever the inputs are doing — it is marked $1$ on the board below.
Then they let the input line knock a single domino sideways out of that
running line's path.

A sideways tile is the second piece of equipment on this page. It does
not pass the fall on to its neighbours. Instead it ejects the tile it
points at, and the gap it leaves stops any run that was coming through.

<DominoDemo client:visible demo="not" initialA={1} />

The output reads "the free run got through", which is $\texttt{1}$
unless the input arrived. Look at what had to be brought in from outside
to make this work: a signal that is $\texttt{1}$ for free. The previous
page said precisely that, in algebra — *the $\texttt{1}$ has to come in
from somewhere.*

There is one more thing to notice, and it is the part that makes domino
computers hard to build in a school hall. Press Step and watch the two
waves race. The knock only saves the day if it arrives *before* the free
run reaches that square. Move the input line two squares further away
and the gate stops working, with nothing visibly wrong with it. In this
medium a gate has to be timed as well as wired.

## AND

Once there is a flipper, everything else follows. The board below is the
"flip the inputs, OR them, flip the result" idea from the previous page,
trimmed down: the free run across the middle is a NOT gate for B, and
its output ends in a knock that ejects a tile from A's path.

So A gets through to the output only when B's flip *failed* — that is,
only when B toppled. A and B both toppling is the only case that lights
the output.

<DominoDemo client:visible demo="and" cellPx={30} initialA={1} initialB={1} />

Notice the long detour A's line takes around the left-hand side. It is
not decoration. A has to arrive at the crossing *after* the block has
had a chance to land, so the extra distance is buying time.

## Build your own

Four tasks, in order of difficulty, and then a blank board. Choose a
piece from the palette and click a square to place it; drag to lay a run
of plain dominoes. The grey squares are fixed — the inputs and the
output stay where they are.

The panel on the right shows what your board actually does on all four
input rows, updated as you build. You do not have to press anything to
be told you are right.

<DominoLab client:visible />

Task 3 is the one worth sitting with. Try it first with plain dominoes
and sideways tiles only, before you reach for the free run, and watch
what happens on the row where A is $\texttt{0}$: nothing moves, so the
output cannot possibly be $\texttt{1}$. That is the wall again, and this
time you are walking into it yourself.
