Before we meet Guarini's puzzle itself, we need to get comfortable
with the piece that stars in it: the **knight**.

A knight jumps in an **L-shape**. Two squares in one direction, then
one square at a right angle to that. It is the only chess piece that
can hop over others, and here we care only about where it lands, never
about what it jumped over.

That is the whole rule and it takes ten seconds to read. Being able to
*see* it on a board is a different thing, and it is what the rest of
this page is for.

## Where can it land?

In each board below, a knight has been dropped on a square. **Mark
every square it can reach in one jump**, then press Check. Nothing is
marked for you, and nothing is revealed until you commit.

Two kinds of square are not landing squares. A **hole** is a place
where the board simply is not there. A **skull** sits on a square that
does exist, and the knight is welcome to fly over it, but it must not
come down on it.

Press *Next square* for a fresh position. The supply never runs out, so
stay here until you have several in a row exactly right.

You will have noticed how uneven the knight is. From the middle of a
board it has eight squares to choose from. From a corner it has two.
That lopsidedness is worth remembering, because it is the reason some
of the boards further down are hard.

## Getting somewhere

Now put the move to work. Click the square you want the knight to jump
to. If it is a legal landing square the knight goes there. If it is
not, the square flashes and your *wrong tries* counter goes up by one.
**You are never shown the legal moves.** That is the whole point of the
page.

Undo takes back a jump and Reset starts the board over. When you reach
the apple you are told the smallest number of jumps the board can be
done in, so there is usually something left to beat.

## An answer of a different kind

A warning before you go on. **Not every board on this page can be
done.** On some of them the apple cannot be reached at all, however
long you fly around, and no amount of patience will change that.

So there is a second way to finish a board. After a few attempts a
button appears offering to let you **say it is impossible**. Calling an
impossible board impossible counts as solving it. Calling a possible
one impossible does not, and the board will tell you so.

Here is a board to try that on. It may be possible. It may not.

## What to take away

The knight is fussy. From some squares it has eight choices and from
others it has two, corners starve it, and a square right next door can
cost three jumps while a square across the board costs two. The same
apple can be easy on one board and out of reach on another with the
same rules, because the **shape of the board** is as much a part of the
puzzle as the move is.

And notice what happened on the boards you called impossible. You did
not check every route, because there are endlessly many routes to
check. You saw a reason. Turning that private certainty into a reason
another person is forced to accept is what the next few pages are
about.
