Every puzzle in this chapter so far has been the same puzzle about
different objects. A crowd arrives that looks far too large to
handle. We find a way to line its members up in a single list. Once
they are in a list, we can deal with them one at a time. David
Hilbert, in a popular lecture of the 1920s, found a way of telling
that story with no formulas in it at all, and it has been the
standard way of telling it ever since. The whole of it takes place
in a hotel.

Each part of the story below comes with an animation. Press **Play**
to watch it, or go one step at a time with **Next** and **Back**.
Under each animation there is a box where you can ask a question of
your own, such as where the guest of room $1000$ ends up.

## A hotel with no last room

The Grand Hotel has one room for every counting number, room $1$,
room $2$, room $3$, and so on without end. The point to hold on to
is the last clause. There is no final room, no room at the end of
the corridor. Whatever room number we name, there is another one
past it.

Tonight the hotel is full. Every room has a guest in it, one guest
to a room, and the manager has hung out the *No Vacancies* sign. In
an ordinary hotel, that sign means that nobody else can be given a
room. There, "every room is occupied" and "nobody else can be taken
in" are two ways of saying the same thing. In the Grand Hotel they
come apart, and the whole of this page is about how far apart they
come. So the sign outside is not quite honest, and an honest one
would have to read

<div style={{textAlign:'center', fontStyle:'italic', margin:'12px 0'}}>
No vacancies, rooms available.
</div>

That reads as a joke now. By the end of the page it will read as a
description.

## One more guest

At midnight a traveller walks in and asks for a room. The manager is
unconcerned. She picks up the intercom and makes a single
announcement to the whole hotel.

<div style={{textAlign:'center', fontStyle:'italic', margin:'12px 0'}}>
Would every guest please step out into the corridor, and then go back in to the room one door along.
</div>

The guest of room $1$ moves into room $2$, the guest of room $2$
into room $3$, and in general the guest of room $n$ into room
$n + 1$. Room $1$ is now empty, the traveller takes it, and
everybody sleeps.

<HotelOneMoreGuest client:visible />

A reader is entitled to object in two places. The first objection
concerns *the guest in the last room, who has no next door to move
into*. There is no last room. The objection is a fact about hotels
we have stayed in being smuggled into a hotel we have not. Every
room number $n$ has a room number $n + 1$ sitting after it, so every
guest has somewhere to go.

The second objection is that *all that shuffling must take forever*.
It does not, because it is not a sequence of moves at all. It is one
instruction, issued once, obeyed by everybody simultaneously. Nobody
waits for anybody, and when the announcement is over the hotel is in
its new arrangement.

That is the story. What makes it a piece of mathematics rather than
a piece of whimsy is that we can check it, and the check comes in
two halves.

1. *Nobody is left without a room.* The guest who was in room $n$ is
   now in room $n + 1$, which is a room of the hotel.
2. *No room is asked to hold two people.* Suppose the guests from
   rooms $m$ and $n$ end up in the same room. Then $m + 1 = n + 1$,
   so $m = n$, and they were the same guest all along.

And room $1$ really is free. A guest arrives in room $1$ only if
$n + 1 = 1$ for some room number $n$, which is to say only if there
is a room $0$, and there is not.

## A bus full of guests

At one in the morning a coach pulls up outside. It is a coach of the
same generous design as the hotel. It has a seat for every counting
number, seat $1$, seat $2$, seat $3$, and so on for ever, and every
seat is taken. The hotel is still full, and now there is an entire
infinite busload on the pavement.

The obvious idea is to do the midnight trick over and over. Shift
everybody one door along and put the passenger from seat $1$ into
room $1$. Then shift everybody one door along again and put the
passenger from seat $2$ into room $1$. Keep going in the same way.
This does not work. The trouble is not that the passengers wait too
long, since the passenger in seat $k$ is admitted after $k$ rounds,
which is a finite wait. The trouble is that the original guests
never come to rest. The guest who started in room $1$ is asked to
move at every single round, and so there is no room that we can
point to and say "this is where that guest spent the night." A
rooming plan has to say where everybody *is*, and this one never
gets around to it.

So the manager must free up infinitely many rooms at once, in a
single announcement. Here is one.

<div style={{textAlign:'center', fontStyle:'italic', margin:'12px 0'}}>
Would the guest in room $n$ please move to room $2n$.
</div>

The guest of room $1$ goes to room $2$, the guest of room $2$ to
room $4$, the guest of room $3$ to room $6$. Everybody ends up in an
even-numbered room, and every odd-numbered room is now standing
empty. That is not one spare room or a hundred spare rooms. It is a
spare room for every counting number, which is exactly as many as
the coach has passengers. The manager turns to the pavement and
calls out.

<div style={{textAlign:'center', fontStyle:'italic', margin:'12px 0'}}>
Would the passenger in seat $k$ please take room $2k - 1$.
</div>

Seat $1$ takes room $1$, seat $2$ takes room $3$, seat $3$ takes
room $5$, and so on up the odd numbers. The two crowds interleave,
one from the coach, one from the corridor, all the way along.

<HotelBusload client:visible />

Now the check, which has the same two halves as before, run over
both crowds at once.

1. *Nobody is left without a room.* The guest from room $n$ has room
   $2n$, and the passenger from seat $k$ has room $2k - 1$.
2. *No room is asked to hold two people.* Two old guests cannot
   collide, since $2m = 2n$ forces $m = n$. Two passengers cannot
   collide, since $2k - 1 = 2\ell - 1$ forces $k = \ell$. And a guest
   cannot collide with a passenger, since $2n$ is even and $2k - 1$
   is odd.

The hotel is exactly full again, with no room wasted. An even room
$r$ is $2n$ for $n = r/2$ and holds an old guest. An odd room $r$ is
$2k - 1$ for $k = (r + 1)/2$ and holds a passenger.

From here on, those two halves are all that a check requires. A
rooming plan is acceptable exactly when

<div style={{textAlign:'center', fontStyle:'italic', margin:'12px 0'}}>
everybody named in it is given a room, and no room is given to two people.
</div>

Everything else on this page is the business of *finding* such a
plan. Once we have one, checking it is two short paragraphs.

## A fleet of buses

At two in the morning it gets worse. A whole fleet arrives, bus $1$,
bus $2$, bus $3$, one for every counting number. Each bus is of the
design we met at one o'clock, with a seat for every counting number,
and on every bus every seat is taken.

The plan from one o'clock deals with one bus and leaves the hotel
full. So we could run it on bus $1$, then run it again on bus $2$,
then again on bus $3$. That fails for the same reason the midnight
trick failed at one o'clock. A guest who is moved at every round is
never anywhere in particular, and we would have no arrangement to
report in the morning.

The move that works is to stop looking at the hotel. The hotel is
not the difficulty. The difficulty is that the crowd outside comes
in an awkward shape, sorted into infinitely many separate buses. So
we ask a smaller question first, whether the whole fleet could be
got into a single bus. It can, and we have already done it.

A passenger of the fleet is identified by two numbers, the number of
their bus and the number of their seat. Let us write $(i, j)$ for
the passenger in seat $j$ of bus $i$. The passengers of the fleet
are therefore laid out in a grid, one column for each bus and one
row for each seat. This is the grid of pairs from
[the frog gets an upgrade](../upgraded-frog/). There we listed the
pairs by going along the diagonals of the grid, so that the list
reaches every pair. The thread visits every pair exactly once. Let
the manager send an empty coach round to the fleet with the
following instruction.

<div style={{textAlign:'center', fontStyle:'italic', margin:'12px 0'}}>
The passenger at position $n$ of the thread takes seat $n$.
</div>

Every passenger gets a seat, because every pair is somewhere on the
thread. No seat gets two passengers, because no pair is on the
thread twice.

The fleet drives away empty, and the manager looks out of the window
at one full coach standing outside one full hotel. That is the one
o'clock problem, word for word. She makes the two announcements of
one o'clock, and by half past two everybody in the fleet is asleep
in a room of their own.

## Reducing to a problem already solved

A mathematician and a physicist are each shown an empty kettle
standing on the floor and asked to boil some water. Both fill the
kettle at the tap, set it on the stove, and light the stove. Then
each is shown a kettle that is already full and already standing on
the stove, and asked again. The physicist lights the stove. The
mathematician takes the kettle off the stove, empties it into the
sink, sets it back down on the floor, and remarks that the problem
has been reduced to one already solved.

We have just done what the joke describes. Faced with the fleet, we
did not look for a rooming plan for the fleet. We emptied the fleet
into one coach, which left us facing the problem of one o'clock, and
then we made the two announcements of one o'clock word for word. The
one difference from the joke is that the emptying was not a detour.
Before any rooming plan can be written, the crowd outside has to be
arranged in a single list, and the coach is that list. When a new
problem arrives, then, the question to ask before "how do I solve
this?" is "can I turn this into something I have already solved?"

The whole night's arrangement can be written in one line. On
[the frog gets an upgrade](../upgraded-frog/) we worked out where the
diagonal thread puts a given pair. The pair $(d, k)$ sits at
position

$$
g(d, k) = \frac{(d + k - 2)(d + k - 1)}{2} + k .
$$

So the passenger in seat $j$ of bus $i$ boards the single coach at
seat $g(i, j)$. A passenger in seat $m$ of that coach ends up in
room $2m - 1$. Putting the two together, the passenger in seat $j$
of bus $i$ spends the night in room

$$
2\,g(i, j) - 1 = (i + j - 2)(i + j - 1) + 2j - 1,
$$

while the guest who was already in room $n$ at two in the morning
spends it in room $2n$. The passenger from seat $1$ of bus $1$ gets
room $1$, the passenger from seat $1$ of bus $2$ gets room $3$, and
the passenger from seat $2$ of bus $1$ gets room $5$. The manager
can print the whole register from a formula.

<HotelReduction client:visible />

The lesson of Hilbert's Hotel is that a full hotel can always be
shifted around to make room for a crowd that can be listed, even a
crowd made of infinitely many lists.
